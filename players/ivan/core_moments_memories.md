# Kiril

The winter I turned eighteen, a brutal fever swept through the clan caves. Even our blood struggled against the mountain chill. Father was bedridden for the first time in my memory, leaving a burden on my shoulders in more ways than one. I pushed myself too hard, hunting for days straight without sleep, refusing help because I believed I had to carry the weight alone.

On the fourth night, deep in the woods, a pack of starving dire wolves—twisted, rabid things driven mad by the valley's rot—caught my scent. Exhausted from fatigue, I missed their ambush. A massive grey alpha lunged out of the brush, tearing into my left shoulder and pinning me into a frozen creek bed before I could even draw my blade.

I tried to shift, to call on the Huntress's gift, but my body was too depleted. As the beast’s jaws snapped inches from my throat, a streak of silver and fury slammed into its side with the force of an avalanche.

It was Kiril. He had tracked me through the blizzard.

He did not shift; he stayed human, wielding two short hunting blades with a terrifying, acrobatic speed I could never match. He danced around the snapping jaws of four dire wolves, moving like lightning on the ice, drawing their attention away from me with sharp, mocking whistles. When one lunged at me, Kiril threw himself directly into its path, taking a vicious bite across his forearm just to drive his dagger into the beast's heart.

Together, back-to-back in the freezing water, we cut them down. When the last wolf fell, we both collapsed into the snow, chests heaving, clouds of steam rising from our bloodied coats.

I looked at his torn sleeve, my chest tight with guilt and anger. "I told you to stay at the camp, Kiril. You could have died."

Kiril wiped a smear of blood from his split lip and let out that sharp, reckless laugh of his—the one that always made the young ones follow him anywhere. He bumped his uninjured shoulder against mine, his yellow eyes bright with fierce, unbreakable loyalty.

"And let you hog all the glory, big brother?" he grinned, gripping my arm to haul me to my feet. "You are too slow, Ivan. Too heavy. You need someone to watch your blind spots, or you will trip over your own honor."

We limped back to the caves together that night, leaning on each other's shoulders, laughing quietly in the dark so we wouldn't wake the elders.

That is the Kiril I carry with me in the mists. Not the traitor who bartered with a vampire, and not the rival who jumped to my birthright—but the brother who bled in the snow to keep me breathing, and the only man who ever dared to tell me I wasn't invincible.

# Skennis

The winter I turned sixteen, Father took me and fourteen-year-old Kiril high up the jagged ridges. We were tracking a massive, scarred mountain buck—an old beast that had survived three winters and knew every scent in the valley.

For six hours, Father forced us to crawl on our bellies through freezing slush, refusing to let us shift. "Your human eyes must learn the hunt before your wolf teeth earn the kill," he growled whenever Kiril shifted impatiently in the snow.

When we finally cornered the buck in a narrow ravine, Father held up a single, scarred hand—the silent command to hold position and wait for the wind to shift.

Kiril did not wait. Fueled by a desperate, burning need to prove himself faster and deadlier than me, he let out a sharp snarl and lunged from the rocks, shifting mid-air into a sleek, silver-furred adolescent wolf. He slammed into the buck’s flank, tearing at its hide with reckless, savage fury. The buck panicked, thrashing violently, its heavy antlers catching Kiril across the ribs and hurling him into a boulder.

Before the wounded beast could bolt into the mists, I moved. I did not shift. Driven by my father’s endless drills, I sprinted silently across the ice, slid beneath the buck’s rearing hooves, and drove my hunting knife straight up into the base of its skull. The animal dropped instantly, dead before it hit the snow.

Silence fell over the ravine, broken only by Kiril’s ragged, bruised wheezing against the rocks. Father walked slowly over to the carcass, looked down at the clean kill, and then turned his cold, pale eyes onto Kiril.

"You hunted like an infected cur," Father spat, his voice devoid of mercy. "Loud. Greedy. Blind to the cost. You ruined the meat, you nearly broke your own spine, and if that had been anything bigger, you would be rotting in the dirt."

He then looked at me, giving a single, sharp nod—the highest praise the old man ever gave. I walked over to help my brother up, offering a hand, but Kiril slapped it away, glaring at me with eyes glowing a fierce, bitter yellow.

# Martha

I was twelve years old on the morning after my first true full moon. I was sitting in the cold mud outside the Inner Section of the caves, my chest heaving, my knuckles raw and bleeding from where I had clawed at the granite walls. I felt dirty, terrified by the sheer, blinding instinct that had overtaken my mind the night before. I hated the heat inside my ribs.

My mother did not bring me bandages or warm clothes. She sat down in the dirt beside me, wearing nothing but a thin woolen shift against the mountain chill, and took my bloody hands in hers.

"Look at me, little cub," she whispered, her voice steady as deep water. "Tell me what you feel."

"I am a monster, Mama," I choked out, refusing to meet her eyes. "The beast wanted to tear the mountain down. I could not stop it."

She reached up and placed two cold fingers squarely between my eyebrows, pressing firmly until my frantic breathing slowed. "Listen to the wind through the pines, Ivan. Does the storm hate the trees it breaks? Does the river hate the stone it wears away? No. They are simply what the Huntress made them to be."

She pulled my hands up to her own chest, letting me feel the slow, deliberate rhythm of her heartbeat. "The blood in your veins is not a cage, my son. It is a river. If you try to build a dam against it, it will shatter you. You do not fight nature. You invite it in, you give it a seat by your fire, and you remind it who holds the leash."

From that morning forward, whenever the moon grew fat or my anger threatened to boil over, I closed my eyes, pressed two fingers to my brow, and found her heartbeat in the dark.

# Emil

Not every memory of my home was carved in blood and ice.

Two summers before my exile, during a rare week when the thick Barovian fog parted to reveal a canopy of cold, brilliant stars, I took fifteen-year-old Emil up to the highest perimeter watch-outpost above the clan caves.

He had spent the day tending to an injured owlbear cub in the woods, his tunic stained with moss and blood, his spirit heavy from a harsh lecture Father had given him about being "too soft for the winter."

I sat on the edge of the cliff, sharpening my blade with slow, rhythmic strokes of a whetstone, while Emil sat beside me, knees pulled to his chest, staring out over the dark sea of pine trees.

"Father thinks I am weak, Ivan," Emil said quietly, picking at a loose thread on his sleeve. "He looks at Kiril and sees a blade. He looks at you and sees a fortress. When he looks at me... I think he just sees a stray."

I stopped the whetstone. I looked at my youngest brother—at the gentle, observant eyes that missed nothing, and the quiet empathy that kept the younger cubs from tearing each other apart during training.

I reached over, grabbed him by the back of the neck, and pulled him into a rough headlock until he laughed, swatting weakly at my arms. Letting him go, I rested a heavy hand on his shoulder.

"Father is made of iron, little brother," I said softly, gazing out toward the distant, shadowy peak of Yester Hill. "Iron is strong, yes. It breaks things. It holds the line. Kiril is fire—he burns hot, and he burns fast. But iron rusts in the rain, and fire eats itself until nothing is left."

I tapped a finger against his chest. "You are the roots under the mountain, Emil. Nobody sees them, and nobody writes songs about them. But without the roots, the whole forest slides into the dark. Keep your soft heart. The pack will need it more than my sword one day."

Emil smiled, leaning his head briefly against my shoulder as we watched the mist roll back in, neither of us knowing how soon that day would come.
